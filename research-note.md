# Delegating implementation, owning the standard of acceptance

While developing MoveOut, I received an agent's report that features and images had been added, but the result did not meet my expectations. The gap was between a completion claim and an acceptable outcome. That experience motivates my position: developers should retain responsibility for defining success and assessing the evidence for it, even when implementation and checks are delegated. This is an argument about how to organise development, not a claim that humans always judge software better than AI.

SWE-bench provides a useful starting point. [Jimenez et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.06770v3) evaluate patches against tests drawn from real repository changes: relevant failing tests must pass, and previously passing tests must remain passing. This gives a stronger basis for judging a repair than an agent's account of its own work. However, the authors acknowledge that execution-based testing alone cannot guarantee reliable generations, including their comprehensiveness, efficiency and readability. My inference is that benchmark success establishes performance against a specified criterion; it does not establish whether that criterion captures everything users need. An image-loading test can pass while the image communicates the wrong idea.

Productivity evidence also challenges reliance on impressions. [Becker et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09089v1) randomly assigned 246 tasks undertaken by 16 experienced open-source developers to allow or disallow AI assistance. With early-2025 tools, tasks took 19% longer when AI was allowed, although participants afterwards estimated that AI had made them 20% faster. The randomised design strengthens the causal inference within this setting, but the narrow population and mature repositories limit transfer to student prototypes. Productivity is also not software quality: the study does not establish that AI-generated features are generally worse. It supports the narrower point that developers' confidence, too, needs checking against observed outcomes.

That qualification matters because the evidence changes. [METR's February 2026 follow-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) suggests newer tools may offer greater benefits, but identifies participation, task-selection and time-measurement problems that make the size of the effect uncertain. The two accounts caution against treating the original slowdown as a permanent verdict. They also support evaluating the actual workflow, including review and rework, rather than borrowing a universal productivity claim. Responsibility includes revising a judgement when its evidence no longer fits.

The strongest counterargument is that agents can generate tests, review code and exercise interfaces, making continuous human inspection an expensive bottleneck. [Grace et al. (2026)](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents), writing for Anthropic, distinguish an agent's transcript from the resulting environmental state. They recommend combining executable checks, model grading and human judgement, with calibration of model graders. This is useful operational guidance, although it is a vendor's practice account rather than a controlled demonstration of effectiveness everywhere. I accept the case for automating routine checks. I would not treat a second agent's agreement as sufficient if both agents assessed the same incomplete criteria.

These sources address different questions. SWE-bench makes a repair claim testable; METR measures effects within a development workflow; Anthropic proposes ways to combine evaluation methods. None alone establishes that a product is worth using. My synthesis is that useful verification needs evidence independent of the completion narrative, not necessarily evidence produced by a human. Developers should be accountable for selecting and revising the criteria, while users help establish whether those criteria represent their needs.

In practice, this means defining promised outcomes before implementation, then choosing evidence appropriate to each one. Agents can propose and execute tests, but assertions should follow the requirements rather than merely reproduce the generated code. Before release, explicit invariants need executable checks, shared behaviour needs deployed interaction checks, and claims about understanding need user observation. For MoveOut, receiving an update and understanding whether a pickup is confirmed are different claims. Passing the first check cannot settle the second.

Review effort should follow the consequences of error, rather than requiring manual inspection of every change. A discovered failure should improve the relevant test, instruction or requirement. The standard I defend is that each material product claim has an identifiable basis and a stated limit. Agents may produce much of that evidence; developers remain answerable for deciding whether it supports acceptance.
